How it works

Plain words, real mechanics

No neuroscience needed. Each card explains one piece of what happens between your click and the fly’s reaction.

A map of every wire

Scientists sliced a fly’s brain into thousands of layers, photographed each with an electron microscope and traced every neuron and every contact between them. That wiring map is the connectome: 138,639 neurons and 15 million connections for the female, 165,122 neurons for the male, whose map also covers the nerve cord.

Each neuron is a leaky bucket

Incoming signals pour charge in, and it slowly leaks out. When the level reaches −45 millivolts, the neuron fires a spike and empties back to −52. That is the whole neuron model — “leaky integrate-and-fire” from Shiu et al., Nature 2024 — and all of them follow it.

The wiring decides

A spike reaches the neuron’s partners 1.8 ms later. How hard it pushes depends on how many synapses join the two cells; which way — excite or calm — on the chemical the sender uses. Nobody tuned a single connection: they come straight from the map.

A loop every 20 milliseconds

The page works out what the fly senses — sugar under its mouth, air on its antennae, a shadow growing in one eye — and turns it into spikes in the matching sensory neurons. The brain runs 20 ms in 0.1 ms steps; then the page reads a handful of output neurons and moves the fly. And again.

Only busy neurons cost time

Updating every neuron 10,000 times a second is too slow for a browser. So a neuron is only updated when a spike reaches it: an exact formula jumps it forward and predicts when — if ever — it will fire. A quiet brain costs nothing, and the result matches the Python version to a correlation of 0.9996.

Proof it is the wiring

Tick “Shuffle the wiring” and every neuron keeps its synapses, but they now lead to random cells. Same neurons, same numbers, a random map — and the fly no longer eats, grooms or escapes. The behaviour lives in the connections.
